Track record

Slow to price it in.

If markets took in public information instantly, a company's share price would jump on the day a record went public and then stop. We follow every signal day by day to test whether small company shares keep moving for days afterwards. Every signal counts, not just the good calls.

How we measure it

  • Starting point: the last closing price before the record became public: the day a contract notice was published, or a trial registry record was updated. From then on, anyone could have known.
  • Every day for 20 trading days: we follow the company's price, so you can see whether the move happened at once or kept going.
  • When RiverScan flagged it: we also note how many trading days after the record went public our signal appeared, and how much of the move was still to come after that.
  • Against the market: we subtract the move in a benchmark index over the same days, so a rising market doesn't flatter the result. Biotechs are compared with the FTSE AIM All-Share and contractors with the FTSE All-Share.
  • Good news and bad news: each kind of signal has an expected direction. A contract win should help a share price, so it counts as right if the company beats the market. A stopped trial should hurt it, so it counts as right if the company lags the market. A date pushed back counts as bad news and a date brought forward as good news. Signals that could go either way, like posted results or a registry mismatch, are measured but not scored.
  • Every signal: all medium and high signals are included, whatever happened. Nothing is removed after the fact.
  • Averages only where they mean something: a category is shown once it has at least 5 results.

Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results. Share prices move for many reasons, and a signal arriving before a price move doesn't mean it caused it. This is a record of public data and market prices, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything. See our terms and disclaimer.
